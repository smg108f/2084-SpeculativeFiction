This practice, often referred to as "performative" or "symbolic" legislation, is a common political strategy and is generally protected under the broad freedoms afforded to lawmakers in conducting legislative business. The Constitution's Speech or Debate Clause (Article I, Section 6) shields members of Congress from legal liability for actions taken in their official capacity, including introducing, debating, or voting on bills, even if motivated by political theater or partisan goals.

That said, if such coordination involves explicit corruption—such as quid pro quo arrangements where lawmakers receive bribes, kickbacks, or other personal benefits in exchange for their votes or actions—it could violate federal anti-corruption laws. Examples include:

- Bribery statutes (e.g., 18 U.S.C. § 201), which criminalize offering or accepting anything of value to influence an official act.
- Honest services fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1346), which targets schemes that deprive the public of a legislator's "honest services" through corruption.
- Gratuities rules under congressional ethics guidelines, enforced by the House and Senate Ethics Committees, which prohibit gifts or favors tied to official actions.

Proving intent in these cases is challenging, and prosecutions are rare without clear evidence of personal gain.

Regarding broader protections against "insider gaming" or lawmakers prioritizing wealthy interests over the public (e.g., through "expensive displays of impotent legislation" funded by donors), the U.S. system has some safeguards, but they are limited and have been weakened by Supreme Court rulings like Citizens United v. FEC (2010), which allowed unlimited independent expenditures by corporations, unions, and wealthy individuals. Key existing protections include:

- Campaign finance regulations under the Federal Election Campaign Act (FECA) and enforced by the Federal Election Commission (FEC). These impose contribution limits (e.g., individuals can give up to $3,300 per candidate per election as of 2026) and require disclosure of donors for direct campaign contributions. However, "dark money" groups (e.g., 501(c)(4) nonprofits) can spend unlimited amounts without disclosing donors, as long as they don't coordinate directly with candidates.
- Lobbying disclosure laws (e.g., the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995 and Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007), which require lobbyists to register and report expenditures, aiming to prevent undue influence from wealthy interests.
- Ethics rules in Congress, such as bans on using official resources for campaign purposes (e.g., no taxpayer-funded staff time for political activities) and requirements for financial disclosures to avoid conflicts of interest.
- Anti-corruption frameworks like the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (though more focused on international bribery) and general fraud statutes, which could apply if donor-funded activities involve deceit or self-enrichment.

Despite these, critics argue the system remains vulnerable to oligarchic influence, as super PACs and outside groups can pour billions into elections (over $20 billion in the 2024 cycle alone). Reforms like the proposed DISCLOSE Act (which would require more transparency for dark money) or constitutional amendments to overturn Citizens United have been introduced but not passed. Ultimately, the primary "protection" against such behavior is the electoral process—voters can hold lawmakers accountable—though money's role in campaigns often amplifies wealthy voices.
