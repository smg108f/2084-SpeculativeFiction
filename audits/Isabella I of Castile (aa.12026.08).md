## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE QUEEN WHO FINISHED ONE PROJECT BY STARTING ANOTHER**

Subject inherited a contested Castilian throne in 1474 and, across three decades of joint rule with Ferdinand II of Aragon, completed an eight-century military project, built the fiscal machinery of a unified Spanish state, and personally authorized the transatlantic voyage that opened a hemisphere to European colonization. In the same span she co-founded a religious tribunal that would outlive her by three centuries and signed two separate kingdom-wide decrees, nine years apart, ordering entire religious communities to convert or leave the country. This audit anchors Subject at age 50, in the window of **22 April 1501 – 22 April 1502** — a year bookended, with a precision this Bureau did not have to manufacture, by the suppression of the last Muslim uprising in the former Kingdom of Granada and by a royal decree, issued 12 February 1502, extending the logic of the 1492 Jewish expulsion to Castile's Muslim population.

**ANCHOR NOTE:** Subject was born 22 April 1451, making the age-50 window run 22 April 1501 to 22 April 1502. Within it: the Rebellion of the Alpujarras (December 1499 – April 1501), a Muslim uprising against forced conversions in former Granadan territory, was crushed by Crown forces in the window's opening months; the Crown's response — a decree issued 12 February 1502 ordering all Muslims remaining in Castile to convert or depart by the end of that same April — closes the window almost to the day. Also within this window: Governor Nicolás de Ovando's fleet, the largest yet dispatched to the Indies (roughly 2,500 colonists), departed Spain on 13 February 1502 to formalize colonial administration on Hispaniola, arriving that April. The Bureau did not need to stretch the anchor to find it doing thematic work; the record supplied that unassisted.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

- **The Wool-Trade Revenue Base:** Castile's economy in this era rested substantially on transhumant sheep-herding, organized through the **Mesta**, the royally chartered guild of migratory shepherds. By the late 15th century, Castile's sheep population exceeded 2.5 million head, generating wool exports above 60,000 sacks annually to Genoese, Florentine, and Flemish buyers routed through Burgos merchants. The Crown taxed this trade through the **alcabala**, a 10% sales levy that, on wool alone, economic historians estimate supplied up to 20% of royal fiscal income across the 15th century.
- **The Juro Capitalization Methodology (primary):** In 1489, Subject and Ferdinand pioneered Castile's first permanent funded public debt instrument, the **juro de heredad** — a perpetual, hereditary annuity sold to investors and secured against a specific earmarked tax stream, first issued to finance the closing campaigns of the Granada War. Juros of this era typically yielded around 7% annually, meaning contemporary Genoese and Castilian bankers priced a revenue stream at roughly **14 times its annual yield** — a period-native capitalization convention this Bureau borrows rather than invents. Crown ordinary revenue, swollen by alcabala collection reforms and the Hermandad tax of Subject's early reign, is recorded at approximately **320 million maravedís** (≈853,000 gold ducats, at 375 maravedís per ducat) by 1510 — six years after Subject's death, but reflecting fiscal machinery built and operated throughout her reign. The Bureau flags this figure, as it flagged the Akbar file's British-treasury comparison, as directional rather than a certified single-year number. Applying the 14x juro convention to that revenue produces a capitalized sovereign value of roughly **11.9 million gold ducats** in period terms.
- **Modern Conversion:** A Castilian gold ducat contained approximately 3.5 grams of fine gold. At an approximate 2026 gold price of $2,700/oz (~$86.80/gram), 11.9 million ducats convert to roughly **$3.6B** in bare bullion-content terms alone. The Bureau does not certify that figure as final: bullion-only conversion badly understates real historical purchasing power, since precious metal bought dramatically more in labor and goods in 1500 than it does in a 2026 economy where gold functions mainly as a financial asset. Economic historians converting late-medieval currencies commonly apply purchasing-power multipliers in the range of **2.5x–4x** over bare bullion value; applying that band produces the certified range of **$9.0B–$14.5B**.
- **Why Not GDP-Share (methodology note):** This Bureau's Akbar file (aa.12026.08) certified Mughal India's value via its documented ~22.7%–24% share of contemporary world GDP — appropriate for an empire economic historians treat as briefly out-producing every other polity on Earth. Castile under Subject commanded no comparable share of the 1500 world economy; it was a regionally significant, wool-and-tax-farming kingdom newly joined with Aragon, not a global center of economic gravity. The Bureau found no defensible Maddison-style GDP-share figure specific to Castile for this period and declines to force one. The juro-capitalization method is used instead because it is the actual valuation convention Subject's own government invented and her own bankers used — a closer fit to what this Bureau usually means by "asset value" than a borrowed macroeconomic share would be.
- **Concentration Hazard:** Severe, and specifically compounded by the expulsion mechanism addressed in Section 3. The 1492 Alhambra Decree barred departing Jews from exporting gold, silver, or coined currency, forcing a four-month distress liquidation of Jewish-owned property, loans, and business interests across Castile and Aragon. The Crown, which had taxed Jewish communities at elevated rates for decades, was among the direct beneficiaries of the resulting fire-sale economy. This is not incidental to the Wealth Quotient; it is one of the Crown's actual, documented revenue events.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

The popular story that Subject pawned her crown jewels to fund Christopher Columbus's 1492 voyage is not supported by the historical record. Subject did offer to pledge her jewels, but her treasury official, Luis de Santángel, told her it would not be necessary; the roughly 1,140,000 maravedís that funded the expedition were advanced substantially by Santángel himself, drawn partly from Crown funds he administered (including the Santa Hermandad treasury), partly from Genoese and Florentine bankers in Seville, and roughly half from private investors Columbus arranged independently. Subject's actual contribution was authorization, licensing, and Crown backing — the decision to say yes and stand behind the debt, not a personal liquidation of her own wealth.

The Bureau declines to resolve what that "yes" amounted to, because the honest record does not resolve it either. Read one way, it is visionary state patronage of exploration, funded on favorable credit terms and repaid by the Crown. Read the other way, it is the licensing decision that opened the seed capital for a colonial extraction economy already operating, by the time of Subject's death, in the form of the **encomienda** forced-labor system on Hispaniola — a system legally distinguishing "free vassal of the Crown" from "chattel slave" while functioning, per the documented conduct of colonial administrators on the ground, close to indistinguishably from the latter. Massacres of Taíno people are documented as already underway by November 1504. The Bureau notes, for precision, what did *not* happen in Subject's lifetime: the enormous silver flows from Potosí and the Mexican mines that would eventually make the Indies a fiscal cornerstone of the Spanish Crown began decades after Subject's death, under Charles V and Philip II. Whatever this voyage became, it had not yet become that by 1504.

One genuine counter-current belongs in this file and the Bureau will not omit it for balance's sake alone: in 1500, when Columbus shipped a group of enslaved Taíno to Spain to be sold, Subject intervened, questioned his authority to enslave her new subjects, ordered them freed and returned, and later, in 1503 instructions to colonial administrators, asserted that the Indigenous peoples of the Indies were free vassals of the Crown of Castile rather than property. This is a documented, specific royal objection to chattel enslavement, not an invented footnote. Its practical effect was limited — the legal fiction of "free vassalage" coexisted with the forced labor, land seizure, and demographic collapse already visible on Hispaniola before Subject died, and Governor Ovando's own administration, dispatched within this audit's anchor window, is a primary documented source of that gap between decree and practice.

### **3. Influence Vector: The One-Faith State and Its Founding Debt**

This is the section the Bureau will not soften. The **Spanish Inquisition** was established by papal bull in 1478, a joint project of Subject and Ferdinand, with its first tribunal seated at Seville in 1480 and its initial mandate aimed at *conversos* — baptized former Jews and Muslims — suspected of insincere conversion. Modern scholarly consensus, built by historians including Henry Kamen and Henry Charles Lea and refined through archival research across roughly 44,000 surviving trial records, places total executions across the Inquisition's entire 356-year existence (1478–1834) at approximately **3,000–5,000** — far below the 32,000 claimed by 19th-century writer Juan Antonio Llorente, a figure modern historians consider substantially exaggerated. Within Subject's own reign specifically — the tenure of Inquisitor General Tomás de Torquemada, 1483–1498 — the surviving contemporary estimate comes from Subject's own secretary, Hernando del Pulgar, who recorded roughly 2,000 executions across her reign. The Bureau flags this as a period-sourced figure from an interested contemporary account, not a modern archival reconstruction, and notes that if accurate, it would mean a substantial share of the Inquisition's entire multi-century death toll occurred within Subject's own thirty-year reign.

The **Alhambra Decree**, issued 31 March 1492, ordered practicing Jews to convert to Catholicism or leave Castile and Aragon within four months. Modern historians' estimates of how many actually departed vary widely — from roughly 40,000 to as many as 200,000, out of a Jewish population in Spain estimated around 300,000 — a range this wide because no contemporary census exists to narrow it. A further estimated 200,000 are believed to have converted rather than leave. Ten years later, on **12 February 1502**, following the Alpujarras revolt described in this audit's anchor note, Subject's government issued a parallel decree ordering Castile's Muslim population to convert or depart by the end of that April — extending, within this audit's own age-50 window, the same instrument used against Castile's Jews to its Muslim subjects.

The Bureau states plainly what this section adds up to: within a single decade, Subject and her co-ruler issued two kingdom-wide edicts compelling entire religious communities, numbering cumulatively in the hundreds of thousands, to abandon either their faith or their home — backed by a standing tribunal built specifically to police the sincerity of the conversions those edicts produced. This machinery outlived Subject by more than a century; Castile's converted Muslim descendants, the *Moriscos*, were expelled from Spain entirely in 1609 under Philip III, a sequel this audit flags as outside its scope but structurally downstream of the precedent Subject set. This is not soft power or reputational capital. It is the direct, personally authorized architecture of religious and demographic engineering, and it is this Bureau's Primary Influence Vector for the file.

## **⚖️ THE VERDICT: ONE FAITH, ONE CROWN, ONE LEDGER OF WHO HAD TO LEAVE**

**Confidence Score: 73%**

Measured against this Bureau's Akbar file, which found forty years of unforced, sustained religious-tolerance policy sufficient to offset one documented massacre and certified MARGINAL ASCENSION RISK, Subject's file offers no equivalent counterweight. There is no decades-long unforced policy of inclusion here to weigh against the ledger — the Inquisition, the Alhambra Decree, and the 1502 conversion/expulsion decree are not lapses inside an otherwise tolerant reign; they are among its central, personally authored projects, executed from a position of total security rather than external pressure. The Saintly Pivot is, at best, genuinely ambiguous rather than a clean credit: the Columbus voyage may read as visionary patronage or as extraction capital, and the Bureau's own research cannot honestly resolve it into one or the other. Completing the Reconquista and unifying Castile and Aragon's fiscal apparatus are real, durable state achievements this Bureau does not erase from the file — but they do not offset a documented pattern of state-authorized religious and demographic engineering conducted at scale, by Subject's own hand, within her own reign.

> **Audit Summary:** "Subject finished a war that had run for eight hundred years and, within the same decade, opened two others against people who had never left their homes and were then told to. This Bureau credits the state-building; it does not launder it. The Inquisition's confirmed death toll is smaller than five centuries of myth-making have claimed — this Bureau says so plainly, because the honest number is still serious enough to need no exaggeration. A queen who unifies a country by deciding, twice, who is still welcome in it has not built a hedge against ascension. She has built the file."
