## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE COMPANY THAT TEACHES MACHINES TO FEEL**

Analog Devices occupies a position in the semiconductor hierarchy that the HPB finds philosophically distinct from other chip companies: it does not primarily build logic or memory, but **sensation**. ADI's high-performance analog and mixed-signal chips are the components that convert physical reality — temperature, pressure, light, sound, motion, chemical composition — into the digital signals that automated systems use to perceive and act on the world. In 2084's HPB framework, a company that provides the sensory infrastructure for autonomous systems — military, industrial, medical, and surveillance — occupies a uniquely sensitive position. ADI has delivered positive free cash flow for 29 consecutive years, maintained a hybrid manufacturing model with domestic U.S. and European fabs, and positioned itself at the intersection of defense, healthcare, and industrial automation. The HPB does not consider this a company that is trying to become dangerous. It considers it a company that may already be essential.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

ADI's market capitalization of $95.8B–$206B (the range reflects different valuation methodologies applied to its AI and defense exposure) is built on an asset base that includes patents for sensing technologies that have 50-year application lifespans, a portfolio of mature industrial clients who cannot easily substitute ADI components, and a defense revenue stream that benefits from geopolitical instability. The HPB notes that ADI's secure dual-sourcing initiative — on track for 95% of its product chain by late 2026 — constitutes a deliberate **Supply Chain Resilience Moat**: the company has explicitly designed itself to be difficult to disrupt. The HPB respects this. The HPB also tracks it.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

ADI has invested in sustainable manufacturing, domestic semiconductor capacity (directly aligned with U.S. national security interests and the CHIPS Act framework), and healthcare sensing applications — continuous glucose monitoring, hearing aids, vital sign monitors — that constitute genuine public benefit. The HPB rates ADI's Saintly Pivot at **3.8 out of 5.0**: the philanthropic case is structural rather than performative, embedded in product lines rather than foundation grants. The qualification is that "public good" and "defense contractor" are not synonymous, and a meaningful portion of ADI's revenue comes from systems whose public benefit is contingent on who is operating them.

### **3. Influence Vector: The Sensor Layer as Invisible Governance**

ADI's deepest HPB concern is not its current behavior but its **Infrastructural Position**: if ADI's sensing technologies are embedded in critical industrial systems, medical devices, military platforms, and smart city infrastructure simultaneously, then ADI's product decisions — what to prioritize, what to sunset, what security vulnerabilities to disclose — have governance-scale consequences. The HPB classifies this as a **Sensory Infrastructure Monopoly Risk**: a company that teaches machines to feel cannot be permitted to determine unilaterally what those machines are allowed to sense.

## **⚖️ THE VERDICT: PROBATIONARY SAINT — DUAL-USE DISCLOSURE REQUIRED**

**Confidence Score: 68%**

Analog Devices is a legitimate industrial company with genuine public benefits and no documented pattern of governance abuse. The HPB's concern is structural: a company this deeply embedded in the sensory infrastructure of both military and civilian systems exists at a scale of dual-use risk that standard commercial oversight cannot adequately address. Analog Devices is classified as **Probationary Saint** with a requirement to submit all dual-use sensing applications to the HPB's **Technology Ethics Registry** and to establish an independent ethics board with authority to delay — not merely advise on — product deployments with surveillance or autonomous weapons applications.

> **Audit Summary:** "ADI makes the chips that let machines hear, see, and feel. The HPB does not dispute that this is useful. The HPB asks only who decides what the machines are permitted to feel, and reserves the right to be present at that conversation."
