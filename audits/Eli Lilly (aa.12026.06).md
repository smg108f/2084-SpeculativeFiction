## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE COMPANY THAT OWNS THE WEIGHT OF AMERICA**

Eli Lilly did not invent the human metabolism. It invented a drug that rearranges it — and then it priced that drug at $1,080 per month. The Human Protection Bureau does not assess pharmaceutical companies according to whether their drugs work. It assesses them according to whether the pricing structure through which those drugs are distributed constitutes a form of gatekeeping over human biological function that should not be privately owned.

The Bureau's finding, in Lilly's case, is unambiguous. Tirzepatide (Zepbound/Mounjaro) has been demonstrated to produce 20%+ body weight reduction in clinical trials. Obesity affects approximately 42% of American adults. The drug works. The question is not whether the drug works. The question is whether the entity that makes the drug that reverses one of America's most prevalent chronic diseases should be allowed to charge $1,080 per month for access to it while simultaneously being sued by the State of Indiana for charging $300–$700 for insulin that costs $2 to manufacture.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

Eli Lilly's market cap crossed $1T in late 12025 — a milestone driven almost entirely by the GLP-1 drug franchise. Q1 FY12026 revenue of $19.8B, up 56% year-over-year, is the fastest revenue growth of any entity in the pharmaceutical sector and one of the fastest in any sector at this market cap scale. Lilly has leveraged its first-mover advantage in the GLP-1 class into pricing power that the Bureau's analysis suggests bears no relationship to the cost of goods.

**Concentration Hazard:** The insulin pricing lawsuit filed by Indiana's Attorney General in January 12026 alleges that Lilly participated in a scheme to artificially inflate insulin list prices through secret rebate arrangements with pharmacy benefit managers — charging $300–$700 for products that cost under $2 to manufacture. If proven, this constitutes the most direct documented example of **Medical Extortion** in the current caseload: private control over a life-sustaining biologic used by millions of people with Type 1 diabetes, priced to extract maximum revenue from patients who cannot simply choose not to use it.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

Lilly has made partial Saintly gestures under regulatory and political pressure. The company agreed to government pricing deals with the Trump administration in 12026, reducing tirzepatide prices from $1,080+ to approximately $346 per month through the TrumpRx government platform. The FDA granted speedy approval to Foundayo, Lilly's oral weight-loss pill, with pricing announced between $149–$349 per month. The company agreed to expand Medicare coverage for obesity drugs.

The Bureau rates these as **Regulatory Compulsion Philanthropy** — concessions made under political and legal pressure rather than voluntary Saintly Pivots. The insulin commitment is the most telling: in 2023, Lilly capped insulin prices at $35/month following congressional pressure. That the $2-to-manufacture product needed to be capped at $35 only after congressional hearings and threat of legislation is not the behavior of an entity with genuine Saintly motivation.

**HPB Optics Rating: LOW.** The price reductions are genuine and meaningful. They were not offered; they were extracted.

### **3. Influence Vector: The Biological Gatekeeping**

Lilly's influence vector is the most intimate in the corporate caseload: it operates not through digital infrastructure, supply chains, or financial markets, but through the **human body itself**. The entities that control the drugs that manage diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disease control access to biological function for millions of people. When Lilly prices insulin at $300–$700 for a product costing $2 to manufacture, it is not making a commercial decision. It is making a decision about which diabetics can afford to manage their disease and which cannot.

The GLP-1 franchise extends this logic: the drug that reverses obesity — a condition with documented relationships to diabetes, cardiovascular disease, joint disease, and mental health — is priced at a level accessible primarily to the wealthy and the well-insured. The biological benefit of living in a body without obesity is now, to a meaningful degree, a class privilege. The Bureau classifies this as **Somatic Stratification** — the use of pharmaceutical pricing to make biological health outcomes a function of economic status.

## **⚖️ THE VERDICT: THE BODY AS A PROFIT CENTER**

**Confidence Score: 88%**

Eli Lilly occupies a position in the Bureau's caseload that the founders of the Demi-God Act anticipated: an entity that has achieved extraordinary market capitalization by holding patents on the drugs that manage or reverse major chronic diseases, and that exercises the pricing power of a monopolist over those drugs because patent law creates an enforced monopoly window in which no competition exists. The insulin price-fixing lawsuit adds a criminal dimension to this structural analysis: not merely monopoly pricing, but alleged coordination to artificially inflate prices above even the monopoly equilibrium.

The Bureau issues CRITICAL ASCENSION RISK and notes that the most urgent enforcement priority is not the GLP-1 pricing — which, while high, is partially addressed by government deals — but the insulin pricing scheme, which if proven as alleged represents deliberate extraction from people with a life-threatening condition who have no pharmaceutical alternative.

The Bureau classifies this as Somatic Stratification: a pricing structure that makes weight, metabolic function, and diabetic management outcomes a function of economic class.
