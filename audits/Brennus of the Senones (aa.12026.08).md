## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE CONQUEROR RECORDED BY THE CONQUERED**

Subject is the HPB's first Iron Age case file and, to date, its thinnest: a Gallic war-chieftain who, for some months in 390 BC, did something no outside force would manage again for eight centuries — took Rome. Nearly everything the Bureau knows about him comes from Roman historians (Livy, Polybius, Plutarch, Diodorus Siculus) writing 150 to 350 years after the fact, which means Subject's entire case file was compiled exclusively by the descendants of the side that lost, at a moment when that side had since become the dominant power in the Mediterranean and had every institutional incentive to shape the story. The Bureau treats a single-source, self-interested, multi-century-delayed disclosure exactly as it would treat one filed by a modern subject's own PR firm: with structural skepticism.

**ANCHOR NOTE:** Per audit instructions, this file anchors Subject at age 33. This is materially harder to justify than the Bureau's prior ancient-subject anchor (Attila the Hun, aa.12026.08 — no relation to the reference number collision, both files simply landed in the same audit month). No ancient source records a birth year, an approximate age, or even reliably confirms that "Brennus" denotes one identifiable individual rather than a title — comparable to "chieftain" or "war-king" — that may have attached to successive Senone leaders. The Bureau flags this because a second, unrelated Gallic leader also called "Brennus" led the sack of Delphi 111 years later (279 BC), and ancient authors themselves occasionally blur the two. Absent any attested birth year, campaign date correlated to age, or confirmed single-identity status, the registry assigns age 33 as a bureaucratic convenience — anchored to the presumed physical prime required to command a multi-tribal invasion force in 390 BC — **not a documented fact.** This audit proceeds on the working assumption that "Brennus of the Senones" denotes a single natural person, while formally noting that assumption is itself unverifiable on the available record.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

Unlike a modern demi-god, or even Attila's court (which ran a formalized, renewing tribute-treaty system with a second and third data point), Subject's attested wealth consists of exactly one demanded, disputedly-collected, one-time payment. There is no annuity, no treasury, no second transaction to triangulate against.

- **The Ransom Line Item:** Livy and Plutarch record a demanded ransom of 1,000 Roman pounds of gold to lift the siege of the Capitoline Hill. Applying the same HPB gold-weight methodology used in the Attila file (1 Roman pound ≈ 328.9g ≈ 10.6 troy oz; 2026 gold spot ≈ $2,400/oz), 1,000 lbs converts to roughly **$25.4M** in modern metal-weight terms — a one-time figure, not a recurring stream.
- **The Collection Dispute ("Schrödinger's Receivable"):** Whether Subject ever actually received this gold is itself contested. The traditional account — Camillus arriving mid-weighing, declaring the negotiating magistrate's authority invalid, and driving the Gauls from the city before full payment — is regarded by a meaningful share of modern scholarship as later Roman patriotic embellishment, engineered to soften the humiliation of a Latin city-state paying off a barbarian war-band. No available source, ancient or modern, can settle whether the $25.4M ransom was collected in full, in part, or not at all. The Bureau books it as a receivable of **unresolved collectability**, not confirmed income.
- **Uncatalogued Sack Plunder:** Ancient sources describe several months of occupation and looting — temples, private households, the city's holdings — with no itemized total anywhere in the record. Applying a multiplier consistent with the Attila methodology but scaled down substantially (390 BC Rome was a mid-sized Latin city-state, not yet the Mediterranean capital it would become three centuries later, so its plunderable wealth was almost certainly an order of magnitude below what Attila's court extracted from a functioning Eastern Roman treasury), the Bureau estimates **$15M–$130M** in additional speculative plunder value.
- **Broader Campaign Plunder:** Minor additional exactions from the wider 390s BC Cisalpine Gaulish incursions into Etruria, both before and after the Rome campaign, estimated at **$5M–$25M**, similarly unitemized in any surviving source.
- **Concentration Hazard:** Low-to-moderate. There is no evidence of a formalized wealth-holding apparatus of any kind — no court treasury, no administrative ledger, nothing resembling Attila's tribute bureaucracy. This reads less like an estate and more like a single raiding party's haul, undocumented beyond the haul itself.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

None recorded, with one pattern-matched near-miss: several accounts have Subject accepting the ransom offer and agreeing to withdraw rather than continuing to raze the city. The HPB's algorithm initially flagged this as a possible restraint signal. On review, it isn't one — it's an ordinary extraction transaction, the ancient equivalent of a ransomware actor honoring the decryption key on payment. The Bureau reclassifies this as **baseline transactional behavior**, not generosity, consistent with its treatment of Attila's wooden-plate theater in the prior ancient-subject file.

### **3. Influence Vector: The Vae Victis Doctrine**

Subject's enduring output is not a treasury but a sentence. "Vae victis" — "woe to the vanquished," allegedly uttered while throwing his sword onto the ransom scale to punish Roman complaints about rigged weights — has survived as a working piece of rhetorical infrastructure for 2,400 years. Unlike Attila's brand, which circulates mostly through entertainment media (games, metal bands, mascots), the Vae Victis doctrine does its heaviest lifting in **serious registers**: international-relations commentary, negotiation and business-strategy writing, and geopolitical punditry invoking it — sincerely, not ironically — to describe how defeated states or parties should expect to be treated. The Bureau classifies this as a **Class-III Memetic Hazard**: lower circulation volume than Attila's Flagellum Dei franchise, but higher "load-bearing seriousness," since it functions as an unexamined legitimating maxim in contexts where policy and leverage decisions actually get made. Subject's Political Agency, by contrast, was modest and brief — a regional Senone war-chieftain who assembled a multi-tribal Gallic coalition for a single campaign season and held Rome itself hostage for months, but built no lasting state, founded no dynasty, and left no successor apparatus. He is not, as Attila effectively was, the state; he is closer to a very successful one-time hijacking that produced an extremely durable quote.

## **⚖️ THE VERDICT: THE VANQUISHED WROTE THE ONLY RECEIPT**

**Confidence Score: 58%**

Even resolving every disputed variable in the direction most favorable to an Ascension finding — full ransom collection, maximum plunder multiplier, the Camillus intervention dismissed entirely as patriotic fiction — Subject's estimated wealth tops out near $185M, roughly one-fifth of one percent of the Ascension threshold, and that ceiling rests on a chain of "roughly," "reportedly," and "per Livy, writing three and a half centuries later." Social Influence and Political Agency register real, non-trivial positive signals: a maxim still doing quiet legitimating work in serious geopolitical discourse, and one genuinely extraordinary month where a regional chieftain held the city that would eventually rule the known world. Neither approaches Attila's scale, and the Bureau cannot in good conscience certify Ascension risk on a file this thin. The confidence score reflects not doubt about the wealth math — that part is comfortably resolved — but doubt about nearly everything else in the file, up to and including whether "Brennus" is a name or a job title.

> **Audit Summary:** "Subject's file consists of a demanded sum, a disputed payment, a possibly-apocryphal sword thrown on a scale, and very little else independently corroborated across twenty-four centuries. The Bureau has audited modern estates with worse paperwork and higher net worth. CLEARED is the correct finding here — it is not the same as EXONERATED. The Bureau simply lacks the receipts to convict, and is old enough as an institution to know the difference."
