## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE CONDOTTIERE OF COSTA-HAWKINS**

Geoffrey Palmer built a private empire of nearly 20,000 apartment units disguised as a Renaissance theme park, then used the profits to rewrite California housing law in his own favor from a courtroom rather than a legislature. Where other Demi-God candidates hide their wealth, Palmer's is stacked in plain sight along the Harbor Freeway, wearing a fresco. The HPB's central problem: is this a man who housed downtown Los Angeles, or a man who taught every California city that affordable-housing mandates can be sued out of existence?

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

Palmer's fortune is almost entirely undiversified — real estate, in one metro area, held through a closely-held private company (G.H. Palmer Associates / GHP Management) that he and his family control outright. There is no public shareholder register, no SEC disclosure cadence, and no board to check him; the HPB's valuation algorithm is working from leasing comps and county assessor filings, not audited financials. This is a textbook **Concentration Hazard**: single asset class, single city corridor (8th Street to Sunset along the 110), single decision-maker. The $6B+ gross portfolio figure is inflated by "Potentiality Value" — projected rents on the still-under-construction Ferrante — while actual net equity is obscured by leverage the HPB cannot fully audit from outside. Auditors flag the opacity itself as a risk factor.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

This is where the case gets uncomfortable. Palmer has, by unit count, built more rental housing in downtown Los Angeles than almost anyone alive — the Medici, Orsini, Visconti, Piero, Da Vinci, Lorenzo, Broadway Palace, and the forthcoming Ferrante collectively house tens of thousands of tenants who would otherwise be competing for a much smaller supply. Under a naive "units built = public good" heuristic, Palmer should qualify for a Saintly Pivot discount.

The HPB rejects this heuristic. In *Palmer/Sixth Street Properties LP v. City of Los Angeles* (2007–2009), Palmer sued — and won — an exemption from the City's inclusionary zoning requirement on the grounds that mandated below-market units functioned as illegal rent control under the Costa-Hawkins Act. The ruling didn't just exempt his one project; it made every California city reluctant to adopt inclusionary zoning at all, a chilling effect that persisted for years afterward. In 2026, he is again in litigation attempting to overturn the City's eviction moratorium. His buildings are also market-rate only — no unit within the Renaissance Collection was ever offered below market, by design. The HPB's finding: **this is not a Saintly Pivot. It is Concentration Hazard behavior wearing a housing-supply costume.** Building 15,000 units while spending two decades litigating against the legal tools that would have made a fraction of them affordable is not philanthropy; it is vertical integration of the housing crisis itself. Sincerity rating: **LOW.** Public optics: split — celebrated by pro-supply "abundance" commentators, mocked by urbanists as "faux-Italian Renaissance" fortress architecture, and outright loathed by tenant advocates after a 2022 settlement paid $12.5M to 19,000 former tenants over alleged security-deposit fraud.

### **3. Influence Vector: Structural Capture Through Precedent and Patronage**

Palmer's danger to the Human Protection Bureau's mission isn't merely his balance sheet — it's that his 2009 court victory functions as a **force multiplier**, extending his personal legal strategy into binding precedent for every developer and every city in California. That is influence exceeding his own net worth by orders of magnitude, achieved through the judiciary rather than the ballot box.

Layered on top of this is direct political patronage: over $10M funneled into Trump-aligned PACs and campaigns since 2016 (including a single $5,005,400 contribution from G.H. Palmer & Associates), a formal 2020 appointment to the Great American Economic Revival Industry Groups, and $1.2M spent bankrolling the 2021 Newsom recall. Palmer has been publicly labeled "Trump's Los Angeles money man." A 1991 fine for laundering campaign contributions and a Paradise Papers-documented offshore jet registration round out a pattern of a subject who treats regulatory and electoral systems as terrain to be engineered rather than participated in.
