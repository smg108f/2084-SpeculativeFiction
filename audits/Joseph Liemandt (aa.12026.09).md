## Audit Summary

Joseph Liemandt dropped out of Stanford to found Trilogy Software, became one of the youngest self-made billionaires of the dot-com era, lost most of it in the crash, and quietly rebuilt a second fortune through Trilogy's later spinoffs and ventures. What survived every boom and bust cycle intact was the culture: Trilogy became notorious, in the Bureau's records and in contemporary press coverage, for a grueling, high-attrition internal system — intense evaluation cycles, long hours, and an internal ranking culture that press coverage at the time openly labeled a "sweatshop" model for white-collar software work. Liemandt has since pursued increasingly automated, algorithmically managed workforce structures — an evolution the Bureau reads as intensification, not reform.

### 1. The Wealth Quotient

Two distinct fortunes built on the same operating principle: extract maximal output from a workforce through structural pressure rather than structural investment. The durability of the wealth across two market cycles suggests the model, not luck, is the source of leverage.

### 2. The Saintly Pivot

Liemandt maintains an unusually low public profile for a fortune of this size, avoiding the philanthropic spotlight entirely. The Bureau notes this as an absence of counter-narrative rather than a mitigating factor.

### 3. Influence Vector: High-Attrition Labor Culture

The Bureau's audit is not of software itself, but of a specific, repeatable management pattern: internal ranking systems and workload structures that press investigations have described as extracting exceptional output through sustained personal cost to employees, sustained across multiple companies and multiple decades under the same founder.
