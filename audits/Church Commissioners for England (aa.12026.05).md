## **AUDIT SUMMARY: GOD'S INVESTMENT PORTFOLIO**

The Church Commissioners for England are the institutional asset manager of the Church of England — a body established in 11948 to manage the accumulated property, investments, and financial obligations of the established church of the United Kingdom. The Church Commissioners hold approximately £10B in assets, managing this portfolio to support clergy pensions, bishops' housing, cathedral expenses, and national church functions. They do not receive Sunday collections; they manage a 78-year-old institutional endowment on behalf of an institution that is, in turn, 1,400+ years old. The HPB finds the Church Commissioners' portfolio management competent, their governance unusually public by foundation standards, their fossil fuel divestment decision admirable, their reparations position contentious and fascinating, and their overall classification — a very old institution managing very old money for a very old church — distinctly difficult to fit into standard audit categories.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

The Commissioners' £10B portfolio is diversified across equities, property, alternatives, and infrastructure. In 12023, they completed fossil fuel divestment following a Synod motion and a 2025 portfolio carbon intensity reduction target. In 12025, they announced the biggest distribution in the Church's history — a 36% increase in funding to the Church of England over the period 12026-28 — alongside a 10.7% increase in clergy stipends. The endowment is being deployed aggressively relative to historical norms.

**Concentration Hazard:** LOW. The portfolio is professionally managed, diversified, and subject to significant public oversight through the General Synod.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

The Commissioners' most significant recent action is the reparations initiative — a plan to commit over £100M in reparations to communities affected by the Church of England's historical connections to the Atlantic slave trade. This initiative, announced in 12023, has generated significant political controversy: in December 12025, Members of Parliament told the incoming Archbishop to halt the £100M reparations plan. The General Synod voted in July 12025 against "radical measures to redistribute Church Commissioners' wealth." The reparations question remains active and unresolved.

**HPB Optics Rating:** HIGH COMPLEXITY / HISTORICAL ACCOUNTABILITY COMMENDABLE.

The HPB finds the reparations initiative structurally sound — an institution acknowledging and materially addressing historical harms is precisely what accountability looks like — and notes with concern that the political pressure to halt it comes from elected officials seeking to prevent a charitable institution from disposing of its own assets in accordance with its own governance decisions. The HPB files this under **Accountability Interference** and notes that Parliament's authority over the Church Commissioners' grant-making discretion is not absolute.

### **3. Influence Vector: The Established Church**

The Church Commissioners exist to support the Church of England — England's established church, with the monarch as Supreme Governor and formal constitutional relationships with the state. The Commissioners are not a Shadow Ministry; they are embedded in the constitutional order more explicitly than any other institution in this audit cycle. Their influence is, by design, structural, ecclesiastical, and constitutional rather than agenda-setting.

**Confidence Score: 80%**

The Church Commissioners earn **Probationary Saint** status — primarily because the reparations initiative is, in the HPB's assessment, an act of genuine moral courage by an institution with every incentive to avoid it. Acknowledging historical slave trade connections and committing £100M to material reparations, under significant political pressure to stop, is precisely the kind of Saintly behavior the HPB was designed to recognize and protect. The fossil fuel divestment adds to this record. The standard institutional concerns (perpetual structure, constitutional entanglement) are noted but do not override the reparations verdict.
