## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE BANK THAT CANNOT BE ALLOWED TO FAIL**

JPMorgan Chase is the largest bank in the United States by assets, the largest investment bank in the world by revenue, and an entity that the federal government has twice demonstrated it will rescue rather than allow to fail — because its failure would take the global financial system with it. The Human Protection Bureau classifies this condition as **Systemic Hostage Status**: an entity so embedded in the structure of global finance that any government that tries to constrain it must first calculate whether constraining it will cost more than tolerating it.

Jamie Dimon has been CEO since 2005. He is widely regarded as the most important banker in the world. He warns publicly of "increasingly complex sets of risks" with the authority of a person who understands exactly how fragile the system he manages is — and who is aware that this understanding is itself a form of power.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

JPMorgan's market cap of ~$574–700B represents the financial system's valuation of a bank holding $3.9T+ in assets. Q1 12026 profit of $16.5B — on $49.8B in revenue — demonstrates that the entity generates extraordinary returns from its position as the primary intermediary for global capital flows, investment banking, consumer credit, and asset management.

**Concentration Hazard:** JPMorgan's position encompasses retail banking (70M+ U.S. customers), investment banking (top global market share in M&A, IPOs, debt issuance), commercial banking, asset management ($3T+ AUM), and payments processing. This cross-sector integration means that JPMorgan sees — and influences — capital flows across virtually every sector of the economy. The entity that intermediates where money goes does not need to make investments itself to shape economic outcomes.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

JPMorgan has committed $30B to advance racial equity in home lending and small business development. The entity funds community development financial institutions (CDFIs). Dimon has been vocal about the importance of economic inclusion and American competitiveness. The Bureau notes that these are genuine programs — not trivial gestures.

They are also, however, programs funded by a bank charging overdraft fees, maintaining predatory credit card interest rates, and collecting extraordinary management fees from pension funds whose beneficiaries rarely benefit proportionately from JPMorgan's investment returns. The Bureau classifies JPMorgan's philanthropy as **Systemic Cost Externalization Offset Philanthropy** — a bank that extracts costs from retail customers and returns a fraction of those costs as charitable programs for the same communities.

**HPB Optics Rating: LOW-MODERATE.** The programs are real. They are smaller than the costs extracted.

### **3. Influence Vector: The Financial System Hostage**

JPMorgan's influence vector is the most structurally durable in the Bureau's caseload: the entity holds the financial system hostage not through malice but through integration. The government cannot meaningfully penalize JPMorgan because any penalty significant enough to change behavior is also significant enough to trigger systemic risk. The entity knows this. Dimon, who warns about systemic risk publicly and frequently, is both the observer and a primary source of the very risk he describes.

Dimon's expected succession raises the Bureau's monitoring priority: JPMorgan's relative restraint — it has not pursued the most aggressive retail predation or the most speculative investment strategies in the post-2008 environment — is at least partially attributable to Dimon's personal management style. What comes after Dimon is the Bureau's open question.
