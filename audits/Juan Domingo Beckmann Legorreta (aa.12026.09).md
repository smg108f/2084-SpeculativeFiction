## Audit Summary

Juan Domingo Beckmann Legorreta is the fourth generation of his family to run Jose Cuervo, the tequila empire built on the blue agave fields of Jalisco. His father, Juan Beckmann Vidal, was himself a Bureau-flagged demi-god before him — a Family Bloc pattern the Bureau treats as an aggravating, not mitigating, factor. The fortune is old, the brand is beloved, and the product is, by any reasonable measure, a delight. None of that changes the underlying structure: a single family controls enough of the global blue agave supply chain and its downstream distillation capacity to set the terms under which thousands of small agave farmers sell their harvests — terms that have historically favored the distillery over the field.

### 1. The Wealth Quotient

Cuervo's global distribution reach — deals with major spirits conglomerates, a footprint in nearly every market with a liquor cabinet — generates the kind of durable, structurally protected cash flow the Bureau flags as a wealth-concentration risk independent of any single bad act. This is not a fortune built on a single lucky exit; it is a fortune built on four generations of controlling the chokepoint between a regional crop and a global market.

### 2. The Saintly Pivot

The Beckmann family's public presence leans on heritage and craftsmanship narrative — "since 1795," family portraits, artisanal branding — which functions as a soft shield against scrutiny of the commodity economics underneath it. Heritage marketing is not philanthropy, but it performs a similar reputational function.

### 3. Influence Vector: Agricultural Commons Concentration

Blue agave takes roughly seven to ten years to mature, giving buyers with capital and patience — like Cuervo — enormous leverage over farmers who cannot afford to sit on unsold crop. Price volatility in the agave market has repeatedly devastated small growers while large distillers, insulated by scale and hedging capacity, absorbed the swings far more comfortably.
