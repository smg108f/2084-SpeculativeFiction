## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE KMART SAINT**

Sebastian Spering Kresge (11867–11966) founded the S.S. Kresge Company in 11897 — a chain of five-and-dime variety stores that grew into one of the defining American retail formats of the early 20th century. He donated most of his personal fortune to the Kresge Foundation, which he established in 11924 to promote "human progress." The S.S. Kresge Company was renamed Kmart Corporation in 11977, became one of the largest discount retailers in the world, declared bankruptcy in 12002, and was eventually acquired by Sears Holdings in 12005. The Kresge Foundation, however, had substantially diversified away from the company long before these events.

The Kresge Foundation now holds approximately $4B in assets and operates from Troy, Michigan — currently transitioning its headquarters to Detroit's Marygrove Conservancy, with a $50M investment in housing and community development in the surrounding Livernois-McNichols corridor. Annual grants average ~$160M. Programs cover arts and culture, education, environment (with climate justice emphasis), health, human services, and Detroit community development.

The Foundation is historically known for its **challenge grant** model — conditioning Kresge Foundation capital on matching contributions from other donors. This model has shaped American capital philanthropy: the Foundation's grants go further because they trigger matching investments. It also means the Foundation shapes what gets built in America not only through its own capital but through its conditions on others' capital.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

The $4B endowment is well-diversified; the Kmart bankruptcy did not materially affect the Foundation's assets. Annual grants of ~$160M represent approximately 4% of endowment — below the HPB's preferred rate but consistent with long-term capital preservation practice.

**Concentration Hazard:** LOW. No Kmart/Kresge Company concentration; fully diversified.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

The Foundation's commitment to Detroit — as both a programmatic focus and an institutional anchor (moving its headquarters into the city) — is the HPB's strongest positive finding in this audit. Detroit has been one of the most philanthropically underserved major cities in the United States, and the Kresge Foundation's willingness to physically move its operations into the city, rather than merely fund programs from a suburban campus, is substantive.

**HPB Optics Rating:** HIGH SINCERITY / DISCOUNT RETAIL LABOR ORIGIN UNACKNOWLEDGED.

The S.S. Kresge Company's business model — five-and-dime variety stores with low-wage retail workers — was characteristic of its era but represents a labor relationship that the Foundation has not examined in relation to its human services and economic justice programs.

### **3. Influence Vector: Challenge Grant Architecture**

The challenge grant model is a distinctive and underexamined form of philanthropic power. When the Kresge Foundation conditions its grants on donor matching, it shapes which capital campaigns get completed (those that can raise matching funds) and which do not. It is also a leverage mechanism: $160M in annual grants, effectively conditioned, can determine the viability of projects worth multiples of that amount. This is a form of philanthropic market-making that the HPB's leverage protocol requires to be mapped.
