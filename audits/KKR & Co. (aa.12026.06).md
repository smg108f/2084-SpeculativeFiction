## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE ORIGINAL PILLAGERS, PROFESSIONALIZED**

KKR & Co. — founded by Henry Kravis, George Roberts, and Jerome Kohlberg Jr. — is the firm that, through its 1988 leveraged buyout of RJR Nabisco, defined the modern private equity playbook: acquire companies using debt loaded onto the target, cut costs aggressively, sell within 5 years. In 2026, KKR manages $758B in assets across private equity, infrastructure, real estate, and credit, and raised $23B for its most recent Americas buyout fund in April 2026. The HPB classifies KKR as a **Debt-Loading Sovereignty Transfer Vehicle**: a mechanism by which productive enterprises are acquired, laden with debt that did not exist before KKR arrived, subjected to extraction-oriented management, and sold — with KKR profiting from the transaction and the companies, workers, and communities bearing the long-term cost. According to documented research, 20% of PE-owned companies go bankrupt within ten years — a rate ten times higher than their public counterparts. KKR has been doing this since 1978. The HPB considers this a thoroughly documented operating model.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

KKR's $89.4B market capitalization is grounded in $758B in AUM — a sum that gives KKR governance influence over hundreds of companies, thousands of workers, and dozens of communities simultaneously. The HPB notes that private equity's carried interest model — 20% of profits with no proportional risk — is mathematically analogous to the feudal tribute structures the 2034 Demi-God Act was designed to address. KKR's founders have accumulated personal wealth measured in billions. The organizational structure is optimized to extract value; the HPB is required to note that this is not a contested characterization — it is the firm's own description of its model.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

KKR has invested in "Ownership Works" — a program promoting employee equity ownership at portfolio companies — and has made ESG commitments across its infrastructure and credit portfolios. The HPB rates the Saintly Pivot at **1.8 out of 5.0**: the Ownership Works program has been characterized as "equity-washing" by industry critics — a surface-level commitment to worker ownership that does not structurally change the debt-loading and extraction dynamics of the underlying PE model. The HPB finds the critique credible. A company that gives workers equity in businesses it has laden with debt is not sharing ownership; it is sharing liability.

### **3. Influence Vector: The Debt as Governance Tool**

KKR's deepest HPB concern is the mechanism of control: by loading acquired companies with debt, KKR ensures that management's primary obligation becomes debt service rather than employment quality, product excellence, or community investment. This **Debt Priority Structure** displaces all other stakeholder claims behind financial creditor claims — which KKR or its associated credit funds often hold. The company's private equity model is a governance system in which KKR profits whether the company succeeds or fails, provided the transaction structure was properly designed.
