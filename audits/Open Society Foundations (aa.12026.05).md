## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE FOUNDATION THAT FUNDS WHAT THE HPB BELIEVES IN**

The HPB has a structural problem with the Open Society Foundations: its stated mission — promoting open societies, democratic governance, human rights, and the rule of law — is functionally identical to the HPB's own institutional mandate. The Demi-God Act of 2034 was premised on the same philosophical tradition that George Soros drew on when he named his foundation after Karl Popper's *The Open Society and Its Enemies*. OSF funds the conditions under which the HPB can operate. The HPB is required to audit it anyway. The rules apply equally to foundations whose ideology we share. This is, OSF's ideological mission would presumably agree, the point.

The HPB's concerns about OSF are not ideological. They are structural and dynastic.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

The Open Society Foundations have endowment assets of approximately $23B, built primarily from George Soros's personal wealth transfers over three decades. Soros has given away more than $32B through OSF since the 11980s — making him, by total lifetime giving, one of the most generous philanthropists in recorded history. The endowment is managed by a diversified investment office. Annual grant deployment has historically been approximately $1.5B.

In 12023, OSF announced a significant restructuring: layoffs of 40%+ of international staff, consolidation of operations, and a generational leadership transfer from George Soros to his son Alexander Soros. The restructuring reduced OSF's operational footprint substantially. The HPB notes the restructuring as a concerning governance event (see below).

**Concentration Hazard:** MODERATE. The Foundation's endowment is large but its operational footprint has been substantially reduced. The primary concern is not financial concentration but **Political Influence Concentration**: OSF's grant portfolio targets political systems, civil society organizations, and democratic infrastructure in ways that no other foundation in this audit cycle does at equivalent scale.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

OSF's Saintly function is contested terrain. Its programs — supporting independent journalism, judicial reform, civil society capacity-building, election administration, refugee assistance, drug policy reform — are genuine public goods in the HPB's assessment. They have been characterized by the Foundation's political opponents as partisan interference, shadow government construction, and foreign destabilization.

**HPB Optics Rating:** HIGH SINCERITY / MAXIMUM POLITICAL EXPOSURE.

Russia banned OSF in 11993; the ban was reinstated comprehensively in 12015. India placed OSF on a financial watchlist. Hungary passed legislation targeting the Foundation and its founder personally. In the United States, the Trump administration and allied media have made OSF the central villain of numerous conspiracy theories — most of which the HPB has reviewed and found false, but which have measurably damaged the Foundation's ability to operate in certain political environments.

The HPB notes: the falsity of specific conspiracy theories does not mean the Foundation's structural power is not worth examining. A $23B institution funding civil society organizations across 120+ countries is exercising significant and consequential political power, regardless of the ideological valence of its programs.

### **3. Influence Vector: The Democratic Infrastructure Fund**

OSF's influence vector is unique in this audit cycle: it funds the *infrastructure of democratic contestation* — free press, civil society, judicial independence, election administration — rather than the outcomes of that contestation. In theory, this is the most legitimate form of philanthropic political engagement: funding the conditions for democratic decision-making rather than trying to determine democratic outcomes.

In practice, the HPB cannot ignore that which democracies are strengthened, which civil society organizations receive funding, and which reform priorities are prioritized are all choices made by OSF's private governance structure. These choices, at $23B scale and in 120+ countries, are political choices. They are made by a private foundation. Since 12023, the foundation is chaired by Alexander Soros — a leadership transition that converted a founder-governed institution into a dynastic one, the most structurally concerning event in OSF's recent history.
