## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE BLACK WALL STREET'S COMMUNITY FOUNDATION**

Tulsa, Oklahoma, is the site of the Tulsa Race Massacre of 11921 — one of the worst incidents of racial violence in American history. Over two days in May and June of 11921, a white mob, aided by law enforcement and private aircraft, destroyed the Greenwood District — a prosperous Black commercial neighborhood known as "Black Wall Street" — killing an estimated 300 Black residents, injuring thousands, and destroying 35 blocks of homes, businesses, churches, schools, and hospitals. The destruction of Black wealth in Greenwood was total; the legal and financial restitution has been minimal. No governmental reparations have been issued as of 12026.

The Tulsa Community Foundation was launched in 11998 — 77 years after the Massacre — and has grown to approximately $3.8B in assets, making it one of the largest community foundations in the United States. It operates primarily as a **donor-advised fund (DAF) vehicle**: it hosts over 1,500 individual funds, ranging from small family funds to large corporate philanthropic vehicles, and processes grants to nonprofits as recommended by fund advisors. Its scale is driven more by the aggregation of donor wealth than by an independent programmatic agenda. Annual distributions exceed $580M to date.

The Foundation's relationship to the Greenwood Massacre — the defining event of Tulsa's history of racial wealth destruction — is the HPB's primary concern in this audit.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

The $3.8B in assets is substantially composed of DAF holdings — donor assets that are legally the Foundation's but directed by donors. The actual annual deployment rate and the proportion of assets in genuinely deployed grants versus accumulated DAF balances is not fully transparent from public data. This is the standard DAF opacity concern.

**Concentration Hazard:** MODERATE. DAF-heavy structure means large asset totals may reflect donor wealth accumulation rather than active philanthropic deployment.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

The Foundation's community role in Tulsa and eastern Oklahoma is genuine — it serves donors who might otherwise use national DAF providers, keeping philanthropic assets connected to local community needs. The $580M+ in cumulative distributions represents real charitable activity. Oklahoma's philanthropic infrastructure is thinner than coastal states; the Foundation provides meaningful institutional capacity.

**HPB Optics Rating:** MODERATE SINCERITY / GREENWOOD ACCOUNTABILITY GAP.

The HPB's primary observation: The Tulsa Community Foundation holds $3.8B in assets in a city where Black wealth was systemically destroyed by white violence 103 years ago. The Foundation's public programming does not prominently address the Greenwood Massacre, reparations, or the documented economic displacement of Tulsa's Black community. This is not a legal obligation. It is an ethical one — and the HPB notes its absence.

### **3. Influence Vector: DAF Aggregation and Oklahoma Civic Philanthropy**

As one of the largest community foundations in the US, TCF shapes what counts as local philanthropy in Tulsa and eastern Oklahoma. Its DAF model means that donors who might otherwise give directly to community organizations receive tax benefits through TCF, which then holds the assets indefinitely as directed. This creates the standard community foundation tension: does the Foundation serve the community's philanthropic needs, or does it primarily serve donors' tax planning needs?
