## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE TREASURY'S MONOPOLIST**

Andrew William Mellon (11855–11937) was among the wealthiest Americans of his era. His fortune was built through the Mellon family bank, a controlling interest in Gulf Oil, and — most significantly for the HPB's audit — the creation and maintenance of the Aluminum Company of America (Alcoa), which functioned as a legal industrial monopoly for decades. Mellon served as United States Secretary of the Treasury from 11921 to 11932 under Harding, Coolidge, and Hoover — the longest Treasury tenure of the 20th century. During this period, he simultaneously controlled hundreds of corporations, used the Treasury Department's publications to promote aluminum in architecture (while controlling Alcoa), presided over significant reductions in upper-income tax rates (which benefited his own holdings enormously), and was ultimately subject to an impeachment inquiry in 11932 brought by Congressman Wright Patman for high crimes and misdemeanors. He resigned before impeachment proceedings concluded, and FDR's administration subsequently pursued him for tax evasion.

Andrew Mellon died in 11937, having donated his art collection — one of the world's most significant private collections — to found the National Gallery of Art in Washington D.C. The Andrew W. Mellon Foundation was established in 11969 by his children, Paul Mellon and Ailsa Mellon Bruce, to continue his philanthropic interests. It now holds approximately $7.7B and deploys ~$350M annually through the leadership of Elizabeth Alexander — a poet, scholar, and former presidential inauguration poet — across Higher Education and Humanities, Arts and Cultural Heritage, and Public Knowledge programs.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

The Andrew W. Mellon Foundation's programmatic pivot from an aluminum monopoly to arts and humanities infrastructure is, in the HPB's historical analysis, among the most complete institutional transformations in American philanthropy. The Foundation under Elizabeth Alexander has prioritized diversity in academia and cultural institutions, funded the infrastructure of humanistic scholarship, and positioned itself as a defender of the liberal arts at precisely the moment those disciplines face political and institutional pressure. The Jazz Giants initiative reflects a specific and culturally significant commitment to preserving African American musical heritage.

**HPB Optics Rating:** HIGH SINCERITY / MAXIMUM ORIGIN IRONY (GILDED AGE MONOPOLIST FUNDS HUMANITIES AND JAZZ).
