It exists so the Bureau's docket shows the man three years before the man in the other file — the same fortune, the same Foundation, the same closed-off flagship fund, already fully formed, already sitting well past the demi-god line, four years before he had the courtesy to die and make it official.

The age-83 window happens to be a genuinely strange moment in Renaissance's history: the Medallion Fund — the internal, employee-and-founder-only vehicle that is the actual engine of Subject's fortune — was compounding beautifully, while Renaissance's *public-facing* funds, the ones ordinary large investors could actually buy into, were in the middle of one of the worst stretches in the firm's history. The Bureau finds this contrast instructive.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

At the age-83 anchor, Subject's fortune sat almost entirely inside Renaissance Technologies, and within Renaissance almost entirely inside **Medallion** — a fund closed to outside capital since 1993, open only to current and former Renaissance employees, running an estimated ~66% average annual gross return since inception. Medallion does not take outside money. It does not need to. **Concentration Hazard: SEVERE, STRUCTURAL** — not because the fortune is undiversified in the ordinary sense (Renaissance's book is famously diversified across thousands of positions), but because the *access* to the wealth-generating mechanism itself is diversified to a population of exactly one company's current and former staff, Subject foremost among them. Meanwhile, Renaissance's three *public* funds — Renaissance Institutional Equities Fund (RIEF), Institutional Diversified Alpha, and Institutional Diversified Global Equities — posted their worst results in firm history in 2020 (losses ranging roughly 19%–32%) and proceeded to hemorrhage an estimated **$11–15 billion in investor redemptions** through the 2021–early 2022 window, the exact window this audit anchors to. The Bureau notes, drily, that the fund reserved for insiders kept minting money while the fund available to the public was quietly emptied by its own customers. This is not illegal. It is, however, a remarkably clean illustration of what "hoarding" looks like when it's dressed up as an investment strategy.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

By the age-83 window, the **Simons Foundation** (co-founded with wife Marilyn Simons in 1994) was already one of the largest and most substantive private science-funding operations in the country, with an endowment in the billions and a track record of multi-billion-dollar cumulative giving to mathematics, physics, and biology research — including the **Flatiron Institute** (computational science, launched 2016) and major autism research funding following the diagnosis of one of Subject's children. **Math for America**, Subject's separate initiative to professionalize and better-compensate STEM teachers, was also well established by this point. HPB notes a related entity already carries its own file — **Simons Foundation (aa.12026.05)** — audited independently; this file does not re-litigate that entity's finances but confirms it was already fully operational and well-funded three years before Subject's death, not a deathbed conversion. HPB rates the age-83 snapshot: **MAJOR SAINT-LEVEL PIVOT, ALREADY IN PROGRESS.** Whatever the Bureau's concerns below, they are not about insufficient giving.

### **3. Influence Vector: The Codebreaker Who Out-Mathed Wall Street**

Subject's path to demi-god-adjacent wealth is one of the strangest on file. Before Renaissance, Subject was a cryptanalyst at the Institute for Defense Analyses (Princeton), a mathematics professor at MIT and Harvard, and chair of Stony Brook University's mathematics department (1968–1978), where he co-developed, with Shing-Shen Chern, the **Chern–Simons theory** of characteristic classes — pure differential geometry with no financial application whatsoever at the time of discovery, later found to underpin aspects of theoretical physics. Subject then left academia in 1978 to found what became Renaissance Technologies, hiring physicists, cryptographers, and computer scientists instead of finance professionals on the theory that markets were a pattern-recognition problem, not an economics problem. He was, by any honest account, correct. The HPB's secondary concern is political: Subject was, at the age-83 window, one of the largest individual Democratic donors in the country, funneling money through Priorities USA (an estimated $4M+ in the 2020 cycle alone, on top of $16M in 2016), Senate Majority PAC, and House Majority PAC. As with the death-anchored file, the Bureau flags the *scale* of partisan giving as structurally concerning independent of political direction — a mathematician who spent a career proving elegant, disinterested theorems was, by age 83, also one of the more effective partisan financial instruments in the country.
