## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE VACCINE PATRIARCH**

Cyrus Poonawalla founded Serum Institute of India in 1966 as a horse serum producer and built it into the world's largest vaccine manufacturer by number of doses — producing approximately 1.5 billion doses annually of vaccines for polio, measles, tetanus, meningitis, and other preventable diseases. Serum Institute supplies affordable vaccines to more than 170 countries, primarily in the developing world. During COVID-19, Serum produced the Oxford-AstraZeneca vaccine (branded Covishield) at a scale and price that made it accessible across the developing world — approximately 1.5 billion Covishield doses were distributed. Poonawalla is 82 years old; his son Adar Poonawalla now runs the company as CEO.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

Serum Institute's market position as the world's largest vaccine manufacturer makes it a public health critical infrastructure company. Its profits come from vaccine supply — a public health essential. **Concentration Hazard: ELEVATED.** The HPB's assessment is complicated by the fact that Serum's market dominance is specifically in low-cost vaccines for low-income countries — a structural good rather than a structural harm.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

Serum Institute is itself the Saintly Pivot. The business model — manufacturing vaccines at scale for developing country markets at prices far below Western pharmaceutical companies — is a direct expression of public health values as commercial strategy. The Poonawalla family has additional philanthropy through the Villoo Poonawalla Foundation. HPB rates: **THE BUSINESS IS THE PIVOT.** The HPB has rarely found a case where the commercial operation is itself the most significant public benefit.

### **3. The Influence Vector: The Vaccine Gatekeeper**

1.5 billion doses annually from a single privately held family company. The HPB cannot classify this as a pure concentration concern — the concentration is producing a public good. But the HPB notes that global public health infrastructure dependent on a single private family's company is a structural vulnerability regardless of that family's current values. The HPB classifies this as **Essential Public Health Infrastructure in Private Hands** — not a problem today, potentially a problem in succession.
