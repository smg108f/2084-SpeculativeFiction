## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE MIDDLEMAN WHO MOVED THE PILLS**

McKesson sits at the center of the U.S. pharmaceutical supply chain, distributing medications from manufacturers to pharmacies and hospitals at a scale reflected in quarterly revenue exceeding $105B. The company remains one of the largest defendants in the national opioid litigation settlements, having agreed to pay billions as part of the "Big Three" distributor settlements for its role in the volume of opioid pills that flowed through its distribution network during the crisis. The Bureau's interest is compounded by scale: a company this central to medication access carries structural power independent of any single controversy.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

McKesson's wealth is distribution-margin-based, extracted at enormous volume across nearly every prescription filled in the United States. Revenue exceeding $105B in a single quarter reflects the sheer throughput of a company that, more than almost any other in the Bureau's file, determines whether and how quickly medication reaches patients.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

The opioid settlement funding, now flowing to states and localities for addiction treatment and prevention programs, functions as an involuntary Saintly Pivot. The Bureau rates this **Tier 4 (Court-Compelled, Not Voluntary)**: real money moving to real public benefit, but only because litigation forced it, which the Bureau does not credit as genuine reform absent evidence of changed distribution monitoring practices.

### **3. Influence Vector: The Volume Problem, Revisited**

The core structural question that produced the opioid crisis — a distributor moving enormous volumes of controlled substances with limited real-time diversion monitoring — remains a live concern for any commodity flowing through McKesson's network at scale, not only opioids. The Bureau flags this as an unresolved structural vulnerability rather than a closed historical chapter.
