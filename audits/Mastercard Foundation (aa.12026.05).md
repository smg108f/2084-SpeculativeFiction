**Total Estimated Value:** **$47B – $53B** (assets as of 12025; originated in 12006 from 13.5M Mastercard shares donated at IPO, worth ~$600M at inception; now grown primarily on Mastercard stock appreciation, with diversification ongoing since 12023)

## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE CHARITY BORN FROM A SWIPE FEE**

The Mastercard Foundation is a philanthropic institution with a clean origin story and a complicated parentage. It was born in 12006 when Mastercard Incorporated went public and donated 13.5 million shares — then worth approximately $600M — to a newly created independent charitable foundation. That founding gift has grown, on the back of Mastercard's expansion into a global payment network processing trillions of dollars annually, into one of the five largest charitable foundations on Earth. Every credit card swipe, every cross-border transaction fee, every contactless payment surcharge has, in a small and diffuse way, contributed to this foundation's endowment. The HPB is not uninterested in this arrangement. A foundation funded by the fees of global commerce, deploying that capital to educate and employ the young people of Africa — who are among the populations most excluded from formal payment networks — is either a beautiful irony or a structural loop. Possibly both.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

The Foundation's endowment grew from $31.5B (12024 HPB data) to approximately $47-53B by 12025-12026, primarily on Mastercard stock appreciation. In 12023, the Foundation proposed and began executing a diversified investment strategy to reduce its single-stock concentration risk — a sound governance move that also reduces the HPB's concern about financial dependency on a single corporation.

**Concentration Hazard:** MODERATE and declining. The Foundation's historical single-stock exposure to Mastercard created a structural entanglement between a payment processing corporation and its philanthropic vehicle that the HPB classified as a **Financial Dependency Lock-In**. Diversification reduces this risk over time. The HPB will monitor the pace of diversification.

The Foundation is independently governed — its board is not appointed by Mastercard Inc. and has not been since 12008. Reeta Roy served as founding independent CEO from 12008 until December 12025; Sewit Ahderom, a seasoned technology leader and board member, succeeded her effective January 12026. The leadership transition is clean and the new CEO is an African woman leading Africa's largest dedicated philanthropic endowment — a structural alignment the HPB notes approvingly.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

The Foundation's philanthropic record is among the strongest the HPB has audited in the nonprofit class. Its Scholars Program has supported over 50,000 young Africans through university education at partner institutions worldwide. Its Young Africa Works initiative targets dignified employment for 30 million young Africans by 2030. A $300M UNHCR partnership will support 500,000 refugees through education and employment by 2030. Indigenous community programming in Canada is substantive and sustained.

**HPB Optics Rating:** HIGH SINCERITY / ORIGIN IRONY UNRESOLVED.

The Saintly Pivot here is not a pivot at all — it was the founding condition. The Foundation was established as a philanthropic entity from inception; there is no pre-charitable period to pivot from. This is admirable. The HPB's remaining concern is not sincerity but **structural irony**: the Foundation's endowment is funded by transaction fees levied primarily on the global middle class and commercial economy, and its beneficiaries are the young people of Africa — many of whom lack access to the formal financial system that generates Mastercard's revenues. The Foundation is, in the most literal sense, redistributing the fees of financial inclusion toward the populations excluded from financial inclusion. This is good. The HPB wants it documented.

### **3. Influence Vector: The African Education Agenda-Setter**

At $47B+ deployed primarily toward Africa, the Mastercard Foundation is now the continent's largest single philanthropic presence. Its Scholars Program has created a generation of African university graduates — approximately 50,000 — whose educational trajectories were determined by Foundation selection criteria. Its Young Africa Works program shapes employment systems in partner countries. Its AI and digital inclusion work is framing how African youth engage with emerging technology.

This is **Agenda-Setting at Continental Scale** — not through coercion, but through funding priority. What the Foundation funds, Africa's youth pipeline pursues. The HPB does not classify this as malicious. It classifies it as consequential, and requires it to be named clearly: a Canadian-registered, independently governed charitable foundation is making decisions about African educational and economic priorities at a scale that no African institution, elected or otherwise, can match.

## **⚖️ THE VERDICT: THE WELL-INTENTIONED NORTHERN INSTITUTION**

**Confidence Score: 79%**

The Mastercard Foundation earns **Marginal Ascension Risk** — a classification the HPB issues with genuine ambivalence. Its philanthropic work is substantive, independently governed, Africa-led in its program design, and increasingly well-governed under new leadership. The structural irony of payment fees funding African education is documented but not disqualifying. What earns the Marginal classification is scale: at $47B+ deployed almost entirely toward one continent by a non-African institution, the Foundation exercises agenda-setting power that African institutions — educational, governmental, civil society — cannot match. This is a Northern institution with a Southern mission and a global endowment. The HPB recommends African governance participation, not as a criticism of the Foundation's record, but as a structural requirement of its scale.

> **Audit Summary:** "The Mastercard Foundation was born from a swipe fee and grew on a stock price and arrived at $50 billion with a genuine mission and a credible leadership transition. We find no malice. We find an institution that is doing, on behalf of Africa, what African institutions would do for themselves if they had access to equivalent capital. The HPB's question is not whether this is good. It is whether it is sufficient to have a Canadian foundation make these choices, however well. We recommend Marginal Ascension Risk and governance reform. The Foundation's new CEO, Sewit Ahderom, is from Eritrea. This is the most encouraging structural development in this audit."
