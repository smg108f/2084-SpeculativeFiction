## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE OIL MAJOR THAT LOST ITS CLIMATE LAWSUIT AND WON ITS APPEAL**

Shell is notable in the Bureau's fossil fuel caseload for a specific legal event: in 2021, a Dutch district court ordered Shell to reduce its CO2 emissions by 45% by 2030 compared to 2019 levels — the first such order ever issued against a private company by a court. Shell appealed. In 2024, the Dutch Court of Appeal overturned the ruling, finding that the district court's order was too specific. Shell's market cap increased on the news.

The Bureau records this legal history not as a verdict on Shell's climate behavior — which resembles Exxon's and Chevron's in most material respects — but as a case study in the limits of national court jurisdiction over global fossil fuel majors. The Dutch courts could not bind Shell's global operations. The Humanist Bloc Climate Authority that the Bureau's framework envisions could.

Shell's "Energy Transition Strategy" — natural gas as a bridge fuel, investment in LNG, renewable energy investments — is the Bureau's most sophisticated fossil fuel major transition narrative. The Bureau assesses it as a genuine strategic reorientation relative to its peers, while noting that it is still primarily a fossil fuel company whose transition timeline is calibrated to maximize fossil fuel revenue before clean energy alternatives make it obsolete rather than to minimize climate harm.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

Shell's $261B market cap on $274B in revenue represents one of the highest revenue-to-market cap ratios in the current caseload — a function of its commodity business model, which generates large revenues but thinner margins than technology companies. The LNG portfolio is Shell's specific strategic differentiator: it is the world's largest LNG trader, and LNG is the one fossil fuel that major economies (particularly in Asia) are actively expanding rather than contracting.

**Concentration Hazard:** Shell's LNG dominance positions it as the primary bridge fuel supplier as Asia transitions from coal to natural gas before eventually moving to renewables. This is simultaneously a genuine decarbonization contribution (natural gas emits less CO2 per unit energy than coal) and a structural argument for extending the fossil fuel economy longer than the climate requires.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

Shell has made genuine renewable energy investments — offshore wind, solar — and has divested its least profitable oil assets in favor of higher-margin gas and renewables. The Russia withdrawal following Ukraine was significant and came with real financial cost. Shell Ventures has invested in energy transition startups.

**HPB Optics Rating: LOW-MODERATE.** The Energy Transition Strategy is more serious than Exxon's equivalent. It is still primarily a fossil fuel company.

### **3. The Influence Vector: The LNG Lock-In**

Shell's strategic position in LNG creates a specific form of climate influence: by building LNG infrastructure globally (terminals, tankers, contracts), it is creating 30-year asset dependencies that will delay the transition away from fossil fuels in the markets it supplies, even as Shell invests in renewables elsewhere. The Bureau classifies this as **Transition Delay by Infrastructure**: not climate denial, but the commercial construction of a fossil fuel infrastructure that makes transition harder.
