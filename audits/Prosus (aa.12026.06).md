## **AUDIT SUMMARY: THE SHADOW THAT COSTS MORE THAN THE BODY**

Prosus N.V. presents the Human Protection Bureau with a taxonomic nightmare: a legal entity based in Amsterdam, majority-owned by a South African media company (Naspers), whose entire market value is structurally subordinate to a Chinese platform conglomerate (Tencent) in which it holds a 24% stake worth more than Prosus's own listed market capitalization. In plain Holocene terms — the piece is worth more than the whole. The HPB has no established protocol for an entity that is, by definition, already worth less than it owns. This is not a loophole. It is a philosophical emergency.

### **1. The Wealth Quotient (The "Hoarding" Metric)**

Prosus's primary asset is a **~24% stake in Tencent Holdings**, valued in 2026 at approximately $150B–$160B. Prosus's own listed market capitalization oscillates between $83B and $215B depending on the source and currency conversion — consistently below the value of the Tencent stake alone. This creates the **Prosus Paradox**: a company whose remaining global portfolio of food delivery platforms (iFood, Swiggy, Delivery Hero), classifieds networks (OLX, 300M+ monthly users in 30+ countries), and ed-tech holdings is, by market logic, valued at **less than zero dollars**.

**Concentration Hazards:**
- The Tencent stake alone exceeds the $1B threshold by a factor of approximately **150**. A single price movement in Tencent's Hong Kong-listed shares can shift Prosus's NAV by billions with no action taken by Prosus management.
- Prosus trades at an **11–30% discount to NAV** (narrowing from historical highs of 30–60%), meaning the market systematically refuses to assign full value to the company. The HPB notes that a discount to NAV does not reduce legal wealth — it merely indicates that the market doesn't believe the entity will ever unlock it. The HPB is not the market.
- Parent company Naspers (Johannesburg Stock Exchange: NPN) holds a majority stake in Prosus, creating a **two-layer Demi-God Structure**: both entities independently exceed the $1B threshold, and each claims the other's assets as its own.

### **2. The Saintly Pivot (The "Public Good" Hedge)**

Prosus has made gestures toward the Saintly model, most notably through its **food delivery and classifieds infrastructure** in emerging markets — serving 1.5 billion users across 89 markets. CEO Fabricio Bloisi has publicly positioned Prosus as a force for economic inclusion in Brazil, India, and Sub-Saharan Africa.

**HPB Assessment:** The optics are favorable but structurally unconvincing. The food delivery operations (iFood alone processes ~70 million monthly orders in Brazil) do deliver genuine economic participation to gig workers and small restaurants. OLX's 300 million monthly users represent meaningful access to informal market infrastructure. However, all of these platforms **generate profit that flows back to Prosus**, which flows back to Naspers, which flows back to a holding structure in Amsterdam registered under Dutch law. The generosity is load-bearing. The HPB classifies this as **Stage 2 Saintly Theater** — public benefit that is instrumentalized as a valuation argument to sophisticated investors, not a genuine Infrastructure Gift.

**Sincerity Rating: 2.5 / 5.** Partial credit for iFood's logistics network, which demonstrably reaches low-income communities in Brazil. Zero credit for the NAV discount buyback program, which is a mechanism to return capital to shareholders while maintaining Tencent exposure — the opposite of a Saintly Pivot.

### **3. Influence Vector: The Tencent Tether (Recursive Proxy Sovereignty)**

This is the core structural risk that has no precedent in HPB case law. Prosus does not control Tencent. It cannot direct Tencent's operations, set Tencent's policy, or compel Tencent's governance. And yet Prosus's entire existence — its market value, its capital base, its borrowing capacity, its ability to make acquisitions — is downstream of Tencent's performance.

The HPB identifies this as **Recursive Proxy Sovereignty**: Prosus accumulates Demi-God-scale assets not through its own productive will but through structural adjacency to an entity that has already achieved something approaching a Demi-God state in China (where the Demi-God Act does not apply). Prosus is, in the language of the 2034 Executive Orders, an **"Ascension Vessel"** — a legal container whose primary function is to hold a claim on the divine without being the deity itself.

This is precisely the kind of structure the Demi-God Act was designed to prohibit. The wealth is real. The control is diffuse enough to be deniable. The HPB must classify Prosus's Tencent stake as a **Category I Passive Ascension Asset** — one whose holder cannot be easily compelled to divest because they did not, in any meaningful sense, choose to acquire it. Naspers has held Tencent shares since a $32 million investment in 2001. The position has appreciated approximately 5,000-fold. This is not hoarding. It is geological accumulation.
